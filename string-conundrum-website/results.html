3Results

What the evaluation showed

Thirty-five students in three cycles, two expert reviews, a supervisor acceptance walkthrough and five technical experiments with criteria fixed in advance. The evidence supports technical correctness and usability with first-year students. It does not yet establish learning gains, and we say so throughout.

0Method

How we evaluated

The human evaluation ran as successive design cycles rather than one summative study: each cycle's findings were fixed before the next ran, so each cycle tested an improved build. Technical experiments checked five measurable requirements against independent ground truth.

  1. 0

    13–18 August 2026

    Peer developer review

    Three developers of PyTutor, a Python-learning tool built by another team, gave unscored, deliberately critical feedback.

  2. 1

    Cycle 1 · 11 students

    First complete build

    Students in their second year of study or later, working remotely at their own pace. It surfaced functional defects and navigation problems.

  3. 2

    Cycle 2 · 11 students

    Testing the fixes

    A similar cohort testing the Cycle 1 fixes, with a looping concept and prompts on explanation quality added.

  4. 3

    10 September 2026

    Supervisor acceptance

    A walkthrough of the production build against a 30-item checklist in seven categories, with a signed statement.

  5. 4

    14–15 September 2026 · Cycle 3 · 13 students

    The target audience

    Run in a UCT Computer Science lab straight after a first-year practical, with auto-marked knowledge questions, a design-claim battery and an issue checklist.

Instruments held constant

  • The ten-item System Usability Scale (SUS)
  • Retrospective confidence ratings per concept
  • An applied knowledge check with objectively markable answers
  • A rating of the isiXhosa support, and free-text feedback

Alongside the cycles

  • Four postgraduate reviewers with HCI experience walked through the interface
  • Five technical experiments, R1 to R5, with success criteria declared before measurement

Ethics

Approved by the UCT Faculty of Science Research Ethics Committee (SCI/03233/2026) with gatekeeper permission from the Department of Student Affairs. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, every participant gave informed consent, and responses were analysed anonymously.

1Usability

“Good” in every cycle, including with first-years

Across the three cycles, 33 participants returned a complete SUS questionnaire. The pooled mean was 80.8 out of 100 (95% confidence interval 76.2 to 85.4), well above the published average of 68, and 26 of the 33 scores reached at least 68. Every cycle fell in the same “Good” adjective band.

The result that matters most is Cycle 3, the only cycle run with the intended audience in the intended setting: a mean of 79.8 overall and 82.5 among the ten self-identified first-years. Usability did not degrade when the audience moved from experienced students to novices, which is where tools like this usually lose ground.

Read these as indicative: the samples are small and self-selected, and internal consistency was lower than in large SUS studies (Cronbach's alpha 0.63 pooled). Cycle 3's median mental-demand rating was a low 3 out of 10.

2Understanding

Immutability is the concept students got wrong

The applied knowledge check is the one instrument that does not rely on self-report. Indexing, comparison and slicing were largely understood. Immutability was not: fewer than six in ten participants gave a fully correct answer about word[0] = "J".

The evaluation's strongest link to the literature

The design rests on the premise that immutability errors are conceptual rather than syntactic, so they persist until the language surprises the learner. Here that premise held locally: the “Jython” model appeared in all three independent cohorts and did not fade with seniority. A learner can index correctly for a year while still believing a string's characters are writeable, because nothing in ordinary practice contradicts the belief.

Slicing errors were just as telling: every one of the five wrong slicing answers was the same off-by-one, ytho for word[1:4], which is the inclusive-stop model the visualiser's “stop (excluded)” label exists to correct.

3Design claims

The visualiser held up best; adaptivity and isiXhosa least

In Cycle 3, each of ten statements tested one design claim, so agreement attaches to a component rather than to the system in general. Both visualisation claims drew no disagreement at all. The lowest-rated items were the two isiXhosa statements and the leaderboard; among the core teaching claims, the weakest was whether practice felt relevant to what a learner was struggling with.

4Technical verification

Five requirements, each checked against independent ground truth

Every experiment paired normal-input checks with malformed, tampered and boundary cases, because “no crash” is not evidence of correctness. Ground truth was external wherever possible: real CPython for the tracer, the challenges' own tests for the transpiler, and a recomputed answer key for practice.

5Expert review

What reviewers and the supervisor said

Acceptance

The supervisor exercised the production build against a 30-item checklist covering all eight modules, Code Space, the visualiser, the four grading types, feedback, localisation, Challenges, the leaderboard and settings, and accepted the system against the project requirements. Sign-up by email link had no verdict recorded. Two comments followed: Code Space should offer a way to clear the editor, and the \n escape should display as one character, which was corrected before submission. The general comment was “Excellent work.”

Criticism, taken seriously

The peer developers were not uniformly positive. All three questioned assessment robustness: marking accuracy, items missing the expected answer, incomplete treatment of negative indexing, too many similar questions, and code-writing items that showed the expected output. The four HCI reviewers converged on navigation and feedback wording, while praising the visualiser's clarity.

Evaluation changed the product

Cycle 1 alone produced eleven changes, each recorded against the finding that prompted it.

One defect, found from two directions

Students in Cycles 2 and 3, and all three peer developers, said they did not always trust their marks. In response, an automated sweep generated 1,600 questions across all eight modules and checked how each answer was graded and displayed. It found that on select-all questions, a correct option the learner had not picked was drawn exactly like one they had picked. Grading was right; the display was not. The fix distinguishes four post-grading states, so only what a learner clicked ever looks selected.

7Honest limits

Limitations and future work

What the evidence cannot show

  • Learning gains. There was no control group and no delayed post-test, and confidence was rated retrospectively, so within-session change cannot be attributed to the system.
  • Generality. Samples were small and self-selected, and participants used two of the eight modules in sessions of about 30 minutes.
  • Comparisons across cycles. Each cycle evaluated a repaired build with partly revised instruments.
  • Correctness beyond scope. The tracer covers a defined Python subset, and sandbox claims are limited to the declared threat model.

What remains to do

  • A controlled pre- and post-test study with a comparison group, over more modules and a longer period.
  • Surface difficulty levels so adaptive practice feels relevant; it was the weakest core claim (mean 3.85).
  • An independent isiXhosa language review: some terms were reported as isiZulu or inconsistent.
  • Validate the fixed exam bank, add rendering tests, help learners know what to do next, and run more Gunicorn workers.

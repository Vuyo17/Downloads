3Results

Results

The evaluation included 35 students in three cycles, two expert reviews, a supervisor acceptance walkthrough and five technical experiments with criteria set in advance. The results support the system's technical correctness and its usability with first-year students. They do not show learning gains.

0Method

Evaluation method

The user evaluation was run in three cycles. Problems found in each cycle were fixed before the next one, so each cycle tested an improved version. Five technical experiments tested measurable requirements against independent reference results.

  1. 0

    13–18 August 2026

    Peer developer review

    Three developers of PyTutor, a Python-learning tool built by another team, gave written feedback and were asked to be critical.

  2. 1

    Cycle 1 · 11 students

    First complete build

    Students in their second year of study or later, working remotely at their own pace. This cycle found functional defects and navigation problems.

  3. 2

    Cycle 2 · 11 students

    Testing the fixes

    A similar cohort testing the Cycle 1 fixes, with a looping concept and prompts on explanation quality added.

  4. 3

    10 September 2026

    Supervisor acceptance

    A walkthrough of the production build against a 30-item checklist in seven categories, with a signed statement.

  5. 4

    14–15 September 2026 · Cycle 3 · 13 students

    First-year students

    Held in a UCT Computer Science lab after a first-year practical, with auto-marked knowledge questions, a design-statement questionnaire and an issue checklist.

Instruments used in every cycle

  • The ten-item System Usability Scale (SUS)
  • Retrospective confidence ratings per concept
  • An applied knowledge check with objectively markable answers
  • A rating of the isiXhosa support, and free-text feedback

Other evaluations

  • Four postgraduate reviewers with HCI experience walked through the interface
  • Five technical experiments, R1 to R5, with success criteria declared before measurement

Ethics

Approved by the UCT Faculty of Science Research Ethics Committee (SCI/03233/2026) with gatekeeper permission from the Department of Student Affairs. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, every participant gave informed consent, and responses were analysed anonymously.

1Usability

Usability

Across the three cycles, 33 participants returned a complete SUS questionnaire. The pooled mean was 80.8 out of 100 (95% confidence interval 76.2 to 85.4), above the published average of 68, and 26 of the 33 scores were 68 or higher. All three cycles fall in the “Good” range.

Cycle 3 was the only cycle run with first-year students in a lab setting. Its mean SUS score was 79.8 overall and 82.5 among the ten students who identified as first-years, which is similar to the earlier cycles with more experienced students.

These results should be interpreted with caution: the samples were small and self-selected, and internal consistency was lower than in large SUS studies (Cronbach's alpha 0.63 pooled). The median mental-demand rating in Cycle 3 was 3 out of 10.

2Understanding

Knowledge check results

The knowledge check was the only instrument that did not rely on self-reported ratings. Most participants answered the indexing, comparison and slicing questions correctly. Fewer than six in ten gave a fully correct answer for word[0] = "J".

Link to the literature

The design assumes that immutability errors are conceptual and not syntactic, so they persist until the learner sees Python reject the operation. The evaluation supports this: the “Jython” answer appeared in all three cohorts, including senior students. A student can use indexing correctly for a long time while still believing that a string's characters can be changed, because ordinary practice rarely contradicts this belief.

All five wrong slicing answers were the same off-by-one error, ytho for word[1:4]. This matches the inclusive-stop misconception that the visualiser's “stop (excluded)” label is meant to correct.

3Design claims

Design statements

In Cycle 3, participants rated ten statements, each about one component of the system. No participant disagreed with either visualisation statement. The lowest-rated items were the two isiXhosa statements and the leaderboard. Among the core teaching statements, the lowest was whether practice questions felt relevant to what the learner was struggling with.

4Technical verification

Technical experiments

Each experiment tested normal inputs as well as malformed, tampered and boundary cases, since the absence of a crash does not show that the output is correct. Results were compared with independent references where possible: CPython for the tracer, the challenges' own tests for the transpiler, and a recomputed answer key for practice.

5Expert review

Expert review and acceptance

Acceptance

The supervisor tested the production build against a 30-item checklist covering all eight modules, Code Space, the visualiser, the four grading types, feedback, localisation, Challenges, the leaderboard and settings, and accepted the system against the project requirements. Sign-up by email link had no verdict recorded. Two comments followed: Code Space should offer a way to clear the editor, and the \n escape should display as one character, which was corrected before submission. The general comment was “Excellent work.”

Criticism

The peer developers also raised problems. All three commented on the reliability of assessment: marking accuracy, items missing the expected answer, incomplete treatment of negative indexing, too many similar questions, and code-writing items that showed the expected output. The four HCI reviewers mainly commented on navigation and feedback wording, and were positive about the clarity of the visualiser.

Grading display defect

Students in Cycles 2 and 3, and all three peer developers, said they did not always trust their marks. An automated check then generated 1,600 questions across all eight modules and tested how each answer was graded and displayed. On select-all questions, a correct option that the learner had not selected was displayed in the same way as one they had selected. The grading was correct, but the display was misleading. The fix uses four separate states after grading, so only the options a learner selected appear selected.

7Limitations

Limitations and future work

Limitations

  • Learning gains. There was no control group and no delayed post-test, and confidence was rated retrospectively, so within-session change cannot be attributed to the system.
  • Generality. Samples were small and self-selected, and participants used two of the eight modules in sessions of about 30 minutes.
  • Comparisons across cycles. Each cycle evaluated a repaired build with partly revised instruments.
  • Correctness beyond scope. The tracer covers a defined Python subset, and sandbox claims are limited to the declared threat model.

Future work

  • A controlled pre- and post-test study with a comparison group, over more modules and a longer period.
  • Show difficulty levels in adaptive practice, which had the lowest rating of the core teaching statements (mean 3.85).
  • An independent isiXhosa language review: some terms were reported as isiZulu or inconsistent.
  • Validate the fixed exam bank, add rendering tests, help learners know what to do next, and run more Gunicorn workers.
